Case study

One number, all the way down.

Hadal is a scroll-driven descent to the bottom of the Challenger Deep, 10,935 m down, for a fictional research institute. Scrolling is diving: one value, depth, drives the colour, light, fog, pressure readouts and sound of everything on screen.

The idea

Depth is the only input.

Most scroll sites animate a timeline. Hadal animates a physical quantity instead. Scroll maps to depth in metres, and every system reads that one number: sunlight falls off, the water loses its colours one by one, pressure climbs past a tonne per square centimetre, the temperature drops toward 1 °C, and the soundscape thins to silence.

Depth isn't linear with scroll, though. The sunlight zone is 200 m deep and the hadal zone nearly 5,000 m. Mapped linearly, the first would flash past in a flick and the last would drag on. So each ocean zone gets the same length of scroll, and depth runs linearly only inside a zone. A persistent HUD shows where you are, with a gauge divided by zone rather than by metre.

Signature moment 01

Watch the colours go.

Water absorbs red first, then orange, yellow and green, until only blue is left. To make that visible, the sub carries a 24-patch colour chart on its bow, and the camera holds on it through the twilight zone. A post-processing pass gives each hue a cut-off depth; past it, that hue loses its colour and falls back to a dim, blue-cast grey. The HUD's spectrum strip drops the same colours at the same depths.

Once the floodlights take over, depth stops being the input. The pass reads each pixel's distance from the camera out of the depth buffer, so colour returns under the lamps and fades a few metres away, which is how it works on a real dive.

Signature moments 02 & 03

Kill the lights. Then map the floor.

In the midnight zone you can switch the floodlights off. In the dark, your cursor (or finger) sets off bioluminescence: short flashes and chains like siphonophores, kept in the 470–490 nm blue that travels furthest in seawater. Ambient flashes keep the dark alive on touch screens.

On the abyssal plain, sonar pings ripple out from under the sub and draw the seafloor as a point cloud with bathymetric contour lines. Then solid terrain fills in behind them as you keep scrolling. The points and the mesh share one heightfield, so the handover is seamless.

Details

Small things that sell it.

  • A real sardine school. 260 instanced fish with mirror-silver flanks that reflect the same water as the backdrop, so they flash only when they roll toward the light. Turns start at the front and ripple back.
  • Light from above. Parallel shafts at the angle sunlight actually refracts into water, fanning out through perspective and swaying as the waves refocus them.
  • A vehicle that's been down before. The sub's panel seams, rivets, stencils and grime are all painted in code, and it gets darker and glossier where the swell keeps it wet.
  • Honest numbers. Pressure, temperature and remaining sunlight are physically based. Light prints as ×10⁻ⁿ %, because that's what it really is.

Engineering

Everything is generated, nothing is downloaded.

There isn't a single image, model or audio file in the experience. Geometry is built from splines and lathes, textures are painted on canvas at load, and the soundscape is synthesised live with Web Audio. The loader's progress bar tracks real build steps rather than fake ones.

It was built to run on a mid-range phone. Touch and low-core devices get fewer particles and lighter geometry, without MSAA. Resolution drops automatically if frames run slow, and throttled background tabs don't count as slow. Sound is off until you turn it on. With prefers-reduced-motion, depth follows scroll without easing, ambient motion freezes and the ascent cuts straight to the end. Without WebGL, a depth-driven CSS backdrop still carries the HUD. Add ?fps to the URL for a live performance readout.

Artistic licence

Where it bends the truth.

In real water, red is mostly gone within a few tens of metres. Here the cut-offs are stretched across 200–1,000 m so the effect plays out slowly enough to watch. The Hadal Trench Institute, its vessel and its observer season are fictional; the depths, pressures and temperatures are not.
